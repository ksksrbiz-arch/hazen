Buying Acreage in Clark County? Check These Details Before You Fall in Love With the View

Start with the parcel, access and property systems. A little homework can turn a promising acreage listing into a much more useful conversation.

Put your plans on paper first

Five acres can look like plenty of room for a house, a shop and a garden. Whether a particular property can support those plans is a different question. Before a tour, write down what you want the land to do for you. Separate what you need now from what you hope to add later.

Be specific. “Room for a shop” leaves a lot unanswered. A rough size, the equipment you want to store and how you would reach it give you better questions to ask. The same applies to animals, a driveway, an addition or a future home.

Make sure you are researching the right property

Ask for the parcel number and confirm which parcels are included in the sale. Clark County’s Property Information Center and MapsOnline provide parcel information and connect with county permit records. Keep the listing, parcel numbers and documents together so everyone is discussing the same property.

Use the map to get oriented. The county says its tax maps are not survey-level accurate and should not be used to locate property lines on the ground. A fence, a mowed edge or a line drawn over an aerial photo should prompt a question rather than settle a boundary.

Give access its own line on the checklist

During a visit, notice where the driveway begins, who appears to use it and how you would move a trailer or equipment through it. Then separate those observations from the documents. Ask what establishes access and whether there are shared-road or maintenance agreements to review.

A road that is easy to drive today does not answer every question about using or maintaining it. If something is unclear, identify who needs to review it before treating the issue as resolved.

Look beyond the house and the scenery

Ask how the property gets water, handles wastewater and receives power. For a septic system, Clark County Public Health provides records resources and lists certified service providers. Available permits, drawings and inspection reports can help organize the next conversation with a qualified professional.

Keep your proposed use in that conversation. An existing system does not, by itself, establish what a future project can support. Ask the relevant agency or provider about the specific plan rather than relying on a broad description such as “utilities available.”

Leave the tour with a short list of unanswered questions

You do not need to solve every technical issue on the first visit. You do need to know which answers are documented, which are observations and which are still assumptions. For each open question, write down the next person to ask and the document or inspection that could help.

Bring Brad the listing link, parcel numbers, your intended uses and that question list. It gives him a useful starting point for helping you decide what to investigate next—and when a surveyor, inspector, utility provider or permitting office needs to be involved.

Choosing Where to Live in Clark County: Compare Your Everyday Life, Not Just the Listing Photos

A good community comparison starts with actual addresses, everyday routes and the same questions at each home. Here is a practical way to narrow your search.

Start with an ordinary weekday

A good lunch and a pleasant walk can make a place easy to like. Before choosing a home, give yourself another kind of visit: the one that looks like your normal day. Where do you need to be, how often do you go there and when would you leave?

Write down a few regular destinations. Use candidate property addresses to test the trips that matter most, at the times you would actually make them. A route from the center of a city is a different test from a route starting at the home you are considering.

Choose two or three places to investigate

Vancouver, Ridgefield, Camas, Battle Ground, Brush Prairie and Washougal give you several starting points. Trying to rank all of them at once can turn a search into a pile of tabs. Pick two or three for your next round of visits, then compare individual properties within them.

Keep the questions consistent: parking, outdoor upkeep, storage, access and the space you use every day. A community name does not tell you whether a particular house has the layout or practical features you need.

Visit the places you would really use

If walking trails matter to you, choose a real trail entrance and try the trip from the property. Ridgefield’s official trail guide, for example, describes city routes and refuge connections. It also identifies access conditions such as seasonal routes and refuge fees. Check the current guidance before building a routine around a place.

Do the same for groceries, appointments or other regular stops. Note the actual entrance, parking and walking connection. A green area or business marker near a home on a map is a reason to investigate, not proof of a convenient connection.

Separate observations from things to verify

Keep three headings in your visit notes: what you liked, what you noticed and what needs an answer. “The driveway is shared” is more useful than “the access seems fine.” It tells you to ask for the relevant documents and understand the arrangement.

For address-specific questions such as school assignment or service availability, contact the responsible district or provider. For property records, begin with the appropriate local agency. Clark County’s research tools are useful for parcel and permit information; keep the source links with your notes.

Compare the home after you compare the location

Picture a normal morning inside each property. Where would you work, keep equipment or bring in groceries? What maintenance would you take on? Which compromises would you still be comfortable with after the excitement of the tour wears off?

Use the community comparison tool to organize a short list, and the buyer checklist to record your priorities. Bring Brad the listings you are considering and the questions left from your visits. The next conversation can focus on actual choices instead of trying to name one community as the answer for everyone.

Getting Ready to Sell in Clark County? Make a Plan Before You Start Spending

Before ordering paint, replacing finishes or scheduling major work, organize your goals, property records and questions. A focused preparation plan starts there.

Start with the move you are trying to make

A seller’s to-do list can grow quickly: paint, flooring, landscaping, repairs, photographs. Before booking the work, step back and write down your timing, the amount of work you can manage and the questions you want answered about the sale.

Bring those details to an early conversation with Brad. A property that needs to be ready soon presents different decisions from one with time for a longer preparation schedule. The first useful question is what needs attention for this property and this move.

Sort the list before you price the list

Use three headings: condition questions, presentation and optional changes. Put something you do not understand—such as a recurring leak—under condition questions. Put clearing a cluttered room under presentation. Put replacing a functioning finish simply because you prefer another style under optional changes.

This is a conversation tool, not a recommendation to leave a problem untreated. It helps you explain what you know and where you need advice. Ask what each proposed project would accomplish, who should evaluate it and how it fits your timing and budget.

Collect records while you plan the work

Make a folder for records you already have: permits, service reports, warranties and information about completed improvements. Note anything you cannot find. Clark County offers online property and permit research, and explains that records not available online may need a separate request.

If the home uses a septic system, raise that early. Clark County Public Health advises sellers and real estate professionals to have a current Report of System Status on file before listing. Check the county’s current guidance and discuss the property with an appropriate certified provider instead of leaving the question until the end of preparation.

Make the property easier to understand

A useful presentation lets a buyer see how the rooms and outdoor areas work. Walk through the home with that purpose in mind. Where does stored material hide space? What would you need to explain during a visit? Which features deserve a clear photograph?

Use the listing-photo guide to plan the views you want to discuss. Photographs should help explain the property accurately. Keep questions about condition and documents on their own list; a good-looking image does not answer them.

Agree on the next few actions

You do not need a long list of projects to have a productive first meeting. Bring a short description of your timing, the work you are considering, available records and the questions you cannot answer. Ask Brad to help you organize the next conversation and identify where specialist advice is needed.

For each action you agree on, record who is handling it and what has to happen before the next step. Use the seller checklist to keep the plan together. The goal is a preparation process you understand, with decisions made deliberately rather than a rush of spending before the listing conversation.
